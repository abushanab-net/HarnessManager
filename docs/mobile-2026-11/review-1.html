01Summary

The phone does three jobs well: see which account has room, find the session that needs you, and answer it while the request is still open. Everything else supports those three. The computer that runs a session stays in charge of every action the phone asks for.

34features, 21 in the first release (MVP)
24screens and sheets, plus the desktop's new “Pair a phone”
6widget surfaces: in-app board, small, medium, large, lock screen, live activity
8pieces of new desktop and hub work before a phone can act
What the desktop must add first. Today the direct link and the hub only carry sync data. Neither lets a phone answer a request, reply or press Stop, and computer pairing hands over the ring recovery key, which a phone must never get. The phone needs its own pairing with scoped grants (summaries, transcripts, control) and a command channel with receipts. These are listed under Desktop work.

Hard limits that shape the design

A question waits for the phone for at most 15 s before it goes back to the terminal; a permission request waits up to 300 s. Reply and Stop need a session started with Harness Manager's own terminal (Windows, setting off by default).

Reachability

At home or on a VPN the phone talks to the computer directly (pinned mutual TLS). Away, it goes through your hub, which only ever sees encrypted data. A sleeping computer means the phone shows the last snapshot and its age.

Your asks, carried through

Gamified but professional, home-screen widgets in the first release (Codex had them in v1.1: moved up, marked ↑ moved), subtle motion, and two distinct high-fidelity directions to choose from.

04Registration and activation

There is no account to sign up for: the phone registers with your computers. A computer shows a QR code, the phone proves it saw the code, and you approve the phone and what it may do on the computer. Through the hub the same approval happens remotely, signed by a computer you already trust.

  1. First installMake a phone key in the Keychain (iOS) or Keystore (Android), excluded from backups. Ask for camera, local network and notifications only when each is first needed.
  2. Invitation on the computerComputers › Pair a phone shows a QR: protocol version, type “phone”, the six-digit code, the address hint and the computer's fingerprint. No recovery key, no admin token, nothing lasting.
  3. Prove the codeThe existing SPAKE2 exchange: two minutes, three wrong tries end it. A wrong code fails at decryption; nothing is revealed.
  4. Approve on the computerThe computer shows the phone's name, three matching words and the grants: Summaries (default), Transcripts, Control. The phone is stored as a phone, never as a full peer.
  5. First sync and notificationsThe phone pins the computer's certificate, syncs, then offers push. The push token is bound to the phone, refreshed when it changes, deleted on revoke.
  6. More computersThe first computer lists the others it knows; each grants access itself. A session on computer B always routes to B.
  7. Revoke or replaceFrom any computer: revoke the grant, close connections, cancel the hub token and push binding. A new phone pairs again with a new key. Data already on a lost phone cannot be recalled, so grants expire if a phone stays offline.

On the same network or a VPN. Reuses the computer pairing's SPAKE2 code exchange with a phone-only payload.

05How it operates

The phone never acts on a stale view: every action names the request and its version, and the computer checks again right before doing it. An action whose result is unknown is never sent twice on its own.

08Gamification

Your brief: fun to use and to monitor, but professional. The rule that keeps it professional: points only for things that save you room or time, never for spending tokens, answering fast or approving more. Every number links to the data behind it, and the whole layer can be turned off.

Motion. Rings fill on open (≈600 ms, eased), the XP bar ticks when you harvest, a single restrained celebration for a level, and a soft pulse only on things that need you. All of it respects Reduce Motion.

  • Most complex to build: two native apps (KMP or SwiftUI + Compose): the most code, but the fewest surprises and the best widgets and motion.
  • Most complex to keep running: MAUI, with four languages in one build. The SwiftUI widget extension and the Swift/Kotlin wrappers sit beside the C#, and every native SDK update touches the bindings.

  • Calendar time: two developers in parallel roughly halve the native options (one per platform); the single-codebase options parallelise less well.

Design brief: the pillars I'll give both designers

  • Glanceable: the answer to “can I keep working, and where?” in under two seconds.
  • Gamified, not childish: rings, streaks and XP rendered with the restraint of a fitness or finance app.
  • Honest numbers: every reading shows its age; estimates carry ≈; unknown is a dash, never a zero.
  • Distinct exteriors: each designer chooses its own type, colour, shape language and layout; neither may copy the desktop app's look one-to-one.
  • Subtle motion: 150–600 ms, spring or ease-out, purposeful; nothing loops except “needs you”.
  • Native feel: iOS and Android conventions (navigation, sheets, haptics) over a shared design language.
